\section*{Summary}

Sweep of the risk-aversion parameter $\gamma \in \{0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 0.8, 1.0,
1.5, 2.0, 3.0, 5.0\}$ at fixed $\tau=1$, on the $G{=}7$ Lobatto--atanh grid.
For each $\gamma$ we run the strict-$h{=}0$ POU+chebroots operator
auto-selecting the best $N_Q$ from $\{12,14,16,18,20,22\}$, and nail the
fixed point with Anderson-accelerated Picard + Newton--Krylov.

\textbf{Methodology recap.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Operator}: strict $h{=}0$, tensor-Chebyshev representation
        of $P(u_1,u_2,u_3)$ on Lobatto--atanh nodes (stretching $c{=}2$).
        Per-cell co-area integral via partition-of-unity over both axes,
        all real roots from chebroots (companion-matrix eigvals in numba).
        $G_p{=}121$ logit-uniform table for $\mu(p, u_k)$ lookup.
  \item \textbf{Solver}: Anderson Type-II with memory $m{=}10$, then
        SciPy \texttt{newton\_krylov} to nail to machine $\varepsilon$.
  \item \textbf{$N_Q$ auto-selection}: try several GL orders, keep the
        FP with smallest $\|F\|_\infty$. Different $(\tau, \gamma)$ have
        different sweet spots.
\end{itemize}

\section{The one-to-one mapping breakdown: linear vs nonparametric deficit}
\label{sec:correct_R2}

The economically meaningful question is: \emph{is the price function
$P(u_1, u_2, u_3)$ a function of $T$ alone?} If yes, $(u_1, u_2, u_3) \to P$
factors through $T = \tau \sum_k u_k$: the price is a sufficient statistic
for $T$ (although not for the individual $u_k$). If no, two signal triples
$(u_1, u_2, u_3)$ and $(u_1', u_2', u_3')$ with $\sum u_k = \sum u_k'$
generally give different prices: \emph{the one-to-one mapping breaks}.

A linear fit $\mathrm{logit}\,P = \alpha T + \beta$ confounds two effects:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item the residual from \emph{nonlinearity} of the true relationship
        (e.g.\ $P = \sigma(\alpha T + 0.1 T^3)$ has nonzero linear residual
        but is still a perfect function of $T$);
  \item the residual from \emph{genuine non-function-of-$T$ structure}
        (different $(u_1, u_2, u_3)$ at the same $T$ giving different $P$).
\end{enumerate}
Only (2) measures the one-to-one breakdown.

To isolate (2), we use a \emph{nonparametric} ANOVA: group the $G^3 = 343$
cells by their (discrete) $T$ value, and compute within-group variance.
At $G{=}7$ there are 63 unique $T$ values and 280 cells share a $T$ with
at least one other cell. The best possible $f(T)$ fit is the per-group
mean; the residual is the within-group variance.

\[
   R^2_{\rm nonparam} \;=\; 1 \;-\; \frac{\sum_{g} \sum_{i \in g}
                           (\mathrm{logit}\,P_i - \overline{\mathrm{logit}\,P}_g)^2}
                          {\sum_i (\mathrm{logit}\,P_i - \overline{\mathrm{logit}\,P})^2}
\]

\paragraph{Economic interpretation revised.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item As $\gamma$ rises (more risk aversion), the deficit shrinks from
        $3.6\%$ to $0.33\%$. The price is becoming \emph{almost} a
        function of $T$ alone --- nearly fully T-revealing.
  \item The "max within-$T$ spread" (largest logit-$P$ difference between
        two cells with the same $T$) ranges from 4.8 (at $\gamma{=}0.1$)
        to 1.6 (at $\gamma{=}5$), in logit units. \emph{Two signal
        triples with the same $T$ can produce prices that differ by a
        factor of $\sim 100\!\times$ in odds at low $\gamma$, but only
        $\sim 5\!\times$ at high $\gamma$.}
  \item The one-to-one mapping $T \leftrightarrow P$ \emph{does} break ---
        but by much less than the standard linear-fit deficit suggested,
        and the breakdown shrinks rapidly with risk aversion.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Why this matters.} For a paper on partial-revelation REE, the
nonparametric deficit is the right metric. The linear deficit conflates
"residual nonlinearity in $f(T)$" (an artifact of the regressor choice)
with "genuine multi-valued price". The former vanishes if you use a
flexible $f$; the latter is the actual economics.
